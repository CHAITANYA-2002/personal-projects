SIH 2026 · Problem Statement 26192 · Ministry of Home Affairs

Slopewatch

A landslide and flash-flood early warning engine for India's hill states — built from public data that has already been verified as reachable, wired through SQL, Python and Power BI, and deployed on free-tier infrastructure.

Superseded — this is the plan as written before the build. It is kept as a record of what was intended, not as a description of what exists. The delivered system diverges in four ways, each for a reason found during the build:
  • MySQL 8, not Postgres with PostGIS. No geometry columns anywhere — distance work is a one-time GeoPandas precompute landed in fact_exposure, which sidesteps the SRID 4326 axis-order trap and keeps the schema portable.
  • No Power BI. One Streamlit application carries all six views, authority-facing and field-facing alike.
  • Negatives are 1:6, not a larger ratio, because the binding constraint turned out to be the weather API rather than statistics.
  • Controls are matched on elevation. The plan did not anticipate that a 5° slope mask admits the Tibetan plateau — the confounder that cost the most to find.

The thesis

What we are actually building

Every part of this project answers one question: given what the sky and the soil have been doing for the last 60 days, which 11-kilometre cell in the Indian hills is most likely to fail in the next three days — and who lives downhill of it?

That second clause is the whole differentiator. A model that outputs a probability is a science-fair project. A system that converts that probability into "these four villages, two bridges and this 9 km stretch of NH-37 need an inspection team today" is a decision-support product, and that is what MHA's problem statement is asking for.

The pipeline below is the spine. Each stage exists because the next one needs it — nothing is bolted on to satisfy a syllabus.

The quiet win

Open-Meteo returns rainfall and four-depth soil moisture from a single unauthenticated call, and its sister flood endpoint returns GloFAS river discharge the same way. That one host replaces the three hardest integrations in the problem statement — NASA GPM for precipitation, NASA SMAP for soil moisture, and a hydrological model for flash-flood signal — with zero credentials and no gigabytes on disk. A ten-year daily series for one location returned 3,653 days in 0.9 seconds.

The correction worth making loudly

ISRO's Landslide Atlas is the dataset every write-up recommends for this problem, and its 80,000-event inventory is real. It is also not downloadable — it lives behind a Bhuvan WebGIS viewer with no bulk or WFS endpoint. Build training labels on it and the project stalls in week one. Use it as a WMS overlay for visual credibility and cite it; take the labels from NASA's catalogue.

What the labels actually look like

The catalogue holds 11,033 global events. 2,037 fall inside the study bounding box, and 1,719 survive the date and country filters. The box spans the whole Himalayan orogen rather than India alone — a deliberate choice, since Nepal and northern Pakistan share the same geology and monsoon regime, and training on the full arc nearly doubles the sample. A trap worth naming

The catalogue stores state names with inconsistent diacritics — Nagaland and Nāgāland are separate values, as are Arunāchal Pradesh and Meghālaya. Grouping on the raw column undercounts Nagaland by 82% and silently drops Darjeeling's events under a second spelling of Bengal. Every count here folds diacritics first, and the study area is selected by bounding box rather than by name — a coordinate cannot be misspelled.

Two properties of these labels shape every downstream decision. First, 89% are rainfall-triggered — 1,518 of 1,719 events are tagged downpour, rain, continuous rain, monsoon or cyclone, against 24 for mining and 22 for earthquake. A rainfall-and-moisture model is not a simplification here; it is the correct physical hypothesis, and the seismic and anthropogenic minority is a documented exclusion rather than a blind spot.

Second, location accuracy is coarse. The median recorded error is 10 km, only 14 events in the whole set are marked exact, and 249 are accurate to no better than 50 km. This is the single hardest fact to design around, and it is why the grid is 0.1° and not finer. Modelling at 100 m resolution against a label that could be 25 km off produces a confident, meaningless map.

Critical path

The decision that determines whether this works

Not the model choice. Not the dashboard. How the negatives are constructed.

There are 1,719 positive events. Everything else in ten years across the study grid is, nominally, a negative — 40,800 cells against 3,653 days is close to 150 million cell-days, and even after the slope mask the negative pool runs to tens of millions. Sample those naively and the model reaches 99.99% accuracy by predicting "no landslide" forever, and the project is dead on arrival regardless of how good the Power BI looks.

Worse, a randomly drawn negative is usually a dry January day in a flat valley. A model trained against those learns to separate monsoon from winter and mountain from plain — both trivially true, neither useful. It will fire on every hill in India every July.

So the negatives are engineered in three deliberate strata:

  1. Temporal controls — same cell, different date. For each event cell, draw non-event days matched on season. This forces the model to explain why this monsoon day failed when 900 other monsoon days in the same cell did not. Highest-value stratum.
  2. Spatial controls — different cell, same date. Neighbouring cells that stayed intact on the day of the event. This removes the "it was raining across the whole state" confound and pushes the model onto terrain and antecedent moisture.
  3. Background controls — random hill cells and dates. A smaller stratum that keeps the model calibrated against the true base rate.

Ratio starts at 1 positive to 15 negatives, with residual imbalance handled by scale_pos_weight rather than further resampling. One guard rail matters: a candidate negative within 15 km and ±2 days of any recorded event is discarded, not labelled zero. The catalogue is a record of reported landslides, and unreported ones are common in remote terrain — labelling a probable event as a confident negative actively teaches the model the wrong thing.

Why this also solves the API budget

Case-control sampling is not a compromise forced by rate limits — it is the standard design in landslide-susceptibility literature. It happens to also be the reason the free tier suffices. Pulling 60-day windows for roughly 27,000 sampled cell-dates costs a few days of background fetching inside the 10,000-calls-per-day allowance, where a full gridded panel would take months. Multi-location batching cuts the request count further, and if a larger panel is wanted later, Copernicus CDS supplies gridded NetCDF in bulk with no per-call limit.

Pillar one

The SQL layer

SQL earns its place here by doing work that would otherwise be duplicated. The rolling-window features the model trains on are the same aggregates the dashboard reports — so they are defined once, in the warehouse, and consumed by both.

Postgres with PostGIS, not MySQL. Two reasons: real spatial predicates make "every road within 5 km of a critical cell" a single query instead of a Python loop, and Postgres window functions handle the rolling and ranking logic that MySQL makes awkward. It is also what Power BI and every free cloud host support cleanly.

The query work worth showing

Ten patterns that carry the analysis. These are not exercises bolted on — each one produces something the model or the dashboard consumes.

  • Rolling rainfall windows — SUM() OVER (PARTITION BY cell_id ORDER BY date ROWS BETWEEN 29 PRECEDING AND CURRENT ROW) for the 1/3/7/15/30/60-day accumulations that drive the model.
  • Soil moisture deltas — LAG() across depth columns to capture how fast the profile is wetting, which matters more than its level.
  • Rainfall anomaly by percentile — PERCENT_RANK() partitioned by cell and day-of-year, so 200 mm in Cherrapunji and 200 mm in Leh are correctly scored as different events.
  • Antecedent precipitation index — a recursive CTE applying exponential decay to prior rainfall.
  • Neighbourhood aggregation — self-join on the 3×3 cell block for spatially smoothed rainfall and neighbour event history.
  • Spatial exposure joins — PostGIS ST_DWithin to attach roads, settlements and facilities within the risk radius of each cell.
  • District risk ranking — DENSE_RANK() over daily aggregated risk, feeding the dashboard's priority table.
  • Month-over-month and year-over-year risk movement — window comparisons for the trend panels.
  • Event-frequency cohorts — landslide rate by state × month × elevation band, the backbone of the historical page.
  • Alert-precision tracking — join predictions back to subsequent observed events to measure how the system performs in production, not just in the notebook.

Evaluation — and why accuracy is not on the list

At a 1:15 sampling ratio a model predicting "safe" everywhere scores 94%. Accuracy is actively misleading here, and quoting it in a viva invites the obvious question. The metrics that matter reflect the real asymmetry: a missed landslide can cost lives, a false alarm costs an inspection vehicle.

MetricWhy it is on the list
PR-AUCPrimary. The only headline number that stays honest under heavy class imbalance.
Recall @ fixed alert budgetThe operational question: if a district can inspect its top 5% of cells today, what fraction of real events did we catch? This is the number to lead with.
Brier score + reliability curveProves the probabilities are calibrated. An officer acting on "80%" needs it to mean 80%.
Lead time distributionHow many hours of warning before the event — the entire point of an early-warning system.
Spatial-block CV scoreGuards against the model memorising known landslide locations rather than learning conditions.
Confusion matrix at operating thresholdThreshold chosen from the false-negative to false-positive cost ratio, and stated explicitly.
Explainability is a requirement, not a garnish

A district magistrate will not evacuate on an unexplained number. SHAP values per cell convert the output into a sentence a human can act on and argue with: "Critical — 88%. Driven by 340 mm over seven days against a 40-year 97th-percentile, saturated upper soil profile, and a mean slope of 38°." That sentence is the actual deliverable of the model.

The deployment trap

Build the warehouse on a local Postgres and Power BI Service cannot refresh it without installing an on-premises data gateway on a machine that stays awake. Start on a cloud Postgres from Phase 00. Migrating later means rebuilding every connection in the report.

Boundaries

What we are deliberately not building

The problem statement describes a national platform: IoT sensor networks, satellite computer vision, telecom SMS gateways, multilingual mobile apps, offline synchronisation. Attempting all of it produces eight shallow demos and no working prediction. Each item below is designed and diagrammed, and honestly labelled as architecture rather than implementation.

  • Physical IoT sensor network — the ingestion schema and API contract are specified; no hardware is deployed. Open-Meteo's modelled soil moisture stands in for sensor readings, and the swap is a single adapter.
  • Deep learning on satellite imagery — terrain and land-cover features derived from satellite products are used; no imagery model is trained. Flagged as the natural version-two extension.
  • Telecom SMS gateway — alert dispatch is built against a webhook, provably working end to end; a licensed SMS provider is a configuration change, not a rewrite.
  • Native mobile application — the Streamlit app is mobile-responsive and handles the geo-tagged reporting requirement.
  • True offline synchronisation — local queueing with deferred upload is demonstrated; full conflict-resolving sync is not.
How to frame this in the viva

Not "we ran out of time", but: "We scoped to the predictive core because a warning system's value is entirely determined by whether the prediction is trustworthy. The integration surfaces are specified and stubbed; the model is validated with spatially blocked cross-validation, which is the harder problem and the one that fails silently if you skip it."
