One

Choosing the problem

01

PS 26192 over PS 26001

MHA over MDoNER

Both ask for landslide early warning. 26001 restricts to the North Eastern Region, which the ground-truth catalogue covers with 351 events. 26192 says "hilly states", which legitimately admits the whole Himalayan arc — and that is 1,719.

The second reason is less scientific and just as real: 26001 is the first entry on the problem-statement list and will be picked by a large number of teams. 26192 sits at position 192.

Rejected26001 — five times fewer labels, many times more competition
02

Verify every source by calling it

before writing any code

Every write-up of this problem recommends ISRO's Bhuvan Landslide Atlas and its ~80,000 mapped landslides. It is real, and it is not downloadable — it lives behind a WebGIS viewer with no bulk or WFS endpoint. NASA's COOLR feature services return HTTP 503. IMD's API answers {"error":"API key missing"}.

Each of those would have been discovered in week two, after the architecture had been built around it.

ResultLabels taken from NASA's Global Landslide Catalog, which is a plain CSV behind a redirect
03

Open-Meteo instead of three separate integrations

the quiet win

The problem statement asks for rainfall, soil moisture and flash-flood signal. The obvious route is NASA GPM for precipitation, NASA SMAP for soil moisture, and a hydrological model for discharge — three archives, two credential systems, gigabytes on disk.

Open-Meteo serves ERA5-Land rainfall and four-depth soil moisture from one unauthenticated endpoint, and GloFAS river discharge from a sister endpoint. A ten-year daily series for one point returned 3,653 days in 0.9 seconds.

CostA hard rate limit, which shaped several later decisions
Two

Defining the study area

04

Select by bounding box, never by state name

reversed a first attempt

The first filter matched state names. It undercounted Nagaland by 82%, because the catalogue stores Nagaland and Nāgāland as separate values — likewise Arunachal Pradesh and Meghalaya, and Darjeeling's events sit under a second spelling of Bengal.

A coordinate cannot be misspelled. Selection moved to a bounding box, and names are only used for reporting, after folding diacritics.

EffectNER 251 → 351 events; study area 797 → 1,719
05

Train on the whole arc, evaluate on India

a config flag, not an accident

The bounding box catches Nepal (481 events), Pakistan (118), Bangladesh, Bhutan, Myanmar and China. Discovering that could have meant filtering them out. Instead it became a deliberate setting: the same orogen and the same monsoon produce the same physics, and training on all of it nearly doubles the sample.

Reporting is always on India. Leave-one-country-out then becomes a real generalisation test rather than a formality.

ReversibleINCLUDE_COUNTRIES = ("India",) restricts to 1,017 events
06

A 0.1-degree grid, and no finer

set by the labels

The temptation is to model at DEM resolution, 30 m. The catalogue's median location error is 10 km, and only 14 events in 1,719 are marked exact. Modelling at 100 m against a label that could be 25 km off produces a confident, meaningless map.

0.1° is roughly 11 km, which matches both the label accuracy and ERA5-Land's native grid — so one cell is one weather pixel, with no resampling inventing precision.

RejectedFiner grids, which would look more impressive and mean less
07

A slope mask on the negative pool

22,594 of 40,800 cells

Drawing negatives from the whole grid would include the Gangetic plain. The model would then learn to separate mountain from plain — true, trivial, and useless, since it would fire on every hill in India every July.

ThresholdMean slope ≥ 5°, computed from the DEM rather than assumed
Three

The sampling design

This is the part that decides whether the project works, and it gets four decisions of its own.

08

Case-control, not a full panel

science and budget agreeing

The full grid over ten years is roughly 150 million cell-days against 1,719 positives. A model trained on that reaches 99.99% accuracy by never predicting a landslide.

Case-control sampling is the standard design in landslide-susceptibility work. It also happens to be the only thing the API allowance can pay for — but it was the right answer before the budget was a factor.

RejectedFull panel; also naive undersampling, which loses the matching
09

Three negative strata, each killing one confound

the core idea

Temporal — same cell, season-matched different date. Forces the model to explain why this monsoon day failed when hundreds of others in the same cell did not.

Spatial — a hill cell 25–300 km away, same date. Removes "it was raining across the whole state": the comparison cell saw the same weather system and held.

Background — random hill cell and date, keeping the model anchored to the base rate.

RejectedUniform random negatives, which are mostly dry January days on flat ground
10

Discard near-misses instead of labelling them zero

15 km, ±2 days

The catalogue records reported landslides. In remote terrain many go unreported. A candidate negative sitting next to a known event in space and time is quite likely a landslide nobody wrote down — and teaching the model that a probable event is a confident negative is worse than not using the sample at all.

Cost46,234 cell-date pairs excluded from the negative pool
11

1:6 rather than 1:15

reversed under the budget

The design started at 1:15. Each sample needs its own multi-week weather window, and the allowance is 10,000 calls a day, so 1:15 meant most rows would never receive weather at all.

A fully covered 1:6 sample beats a 1:15 sample that is 40% empty. Residual imbalance is handled by class weighting, which costs nothing.

Also cutFeature window 60 → 45 days, keeping the 30-day accumulation the literature relies on
Four

Engineering under constraints

12

MySQL, and therefore no geometry in the database

stack was given

Postgres with PostGIS would be the default choice. MySQL was the requirement, and MySQL 8 has everything the analysis actually needs — window functions, recursive CTEs, ranking.

What it lacks is comfortable spatial predicates, and its SRID 4326 axis order is a well-known trap. So no geometry columns at all: distance work happens once in GeoPandas and lands as numbers in fact_exposure. The database never has to be spatial.

BonusThe schema stays portable to any SQL engine
13

Road segments, not polygon overlay

hours to twenty seconds

Attributing a 40 km highway to whichever cell holds its midpoint would burden one cell and spare five. The exact fix is clipping every line against the grid, which on 2.19 million OSM road features takes hours.

Instead the roads are decomposed into vertex-to-vertex segments and each segment goes to the cell holding its midpoint. OSM segments are tens of metres against 11 km cells, so the error is negligible. 29 million segments processed in twenty seconds.

Rejectedgpd.overlay — correct, and unusable at this scale
14

Discharge is stored but not modelled

a leak avoided

Fetching river discharge doubles the API cost. It never entered the top fifteen features, because GloFAS models river reaches and most landslide cells are headwater slopes where it reads near zero.

The tempting compromise — fetch it for some samples and not others — would have been a genuine leak: a feature whose presence tracks fetch order, which in turn tracks stratum. Excluded from the model entirely, kept in the warehouse for the flash-flood reporting.

EffectRemaining backfill halved, from ~6 days to ~2.7
15

Measure the API's billing rather than read it

reversed a guess

The documentation reads as though requests are billed per call, which would make eight-location batching eight times cheaper. That reading was adopted, and the plan said the whole fetch would take one day.

The quota then ran out after exactly 1,385 windows — which is 1,385 × 3.2 × 2 ≈ 8,900 units against a 10,000 allowance. Billing is per coordinate. Batching saves round trips and nothing else.

NowThe cost model matches observed behaviour, and says 2.7 days
Five

Making the output honest

16

Blocked validation, in time and in space

non-negotiable

Weather is autocorrelated over days and terrain is constant per cell, so a random split lets the model score beautifully by recognising cells it has already seen. The failure is silent — every metric looks excellent and the system generalises to nothing.

Train 2007–2013, validate 2014, test 2015–2016. Then leave-one-region-out on top, with Kashmir as its own fold because only 47% of its events are monsoon-driven against 94% elsewhere.

ResultHeld-out-region 0.302 against in-sample 0.294 — it holds
17

Correct the probability scale before showing anyone

four orders of magnitude

Case-control training calibrates to the sampled base rate — one row in six. The real rate is one cell-day in 48,066. The system was ready to report "23% chance of landslide" where the true frequency is 0.003%.

Every prediction now carries two numbers: a relative risk score for ranking, and a prior-corrected absolute frequency for anyone who needs a real probability. They are stored separately and never conflated.

Knock-onBands recut on relative risk, since a "critical at 0.80" rule could never fire
18

Accuracy is not reported anywhere

including in the DAX

At a 1:6 ratio, predicting "safe" everywhere scores 86%. On the real grid it scores 99.998%. The number is not merely uninformative — it actively hides failure, and it is the first thing an audience asks for.

What is reported instead: PR-AUC with a bootstrap interval, recall at a fixed alert budget, Brier score and a calibration curve.

Lead metricRecall at 5% budget — what a district catches if it inspects its top 5%
19

Confidence intervals, after being caught out

reversed a claim

Retraining as coverage grew gave 0.302, then 0.326, then 0.294. The middle number was reported as evidence that more data was helping. It was evidence of nothing: the test split grows with the backfill, so each score was measured on different rows.

Nothing failed. The pipeline ran, the metric moved in the flattering direction, and the conclusion was wrong. Bootstrap intervals now accompany every PR-AUC, and they overlap almost entirely.

RuleNo improvement is claimable until the evaluation sample is fixed
20

Keep the settlement names

the last mile

Exposure counts rank a cell: forty settlements, 56,543 people. They do not tell a team where to go. Joining the risk output back to 75,202 named OSM places turns "forty settlements in Kangra" into Dharamshala, McLeod Ganj, Naddi, Sudher.

A related trap, found the same day: OSM stores population=0 for villages with no census figure. Read literally that is a settlement with nobody in it, which zeroes its exposure and drops it out of every ranking — hiding exactly the small remote places the system exists to protect.

FixA stored zero is treated as missing, not as zero people
The pattern

What these have in common

Four of the twenty were reversals of an earlier choice. None of the reversals came from something breaking — the pipeline ran cleanly through every one of them.

The diacritic filter returned a plausible number. The optimistic billing model produced a plausible schedule. The uncorrected probability produced a plausible percentage. The rising PR-AUC produced a plausible improvement. Each was found by checking a result against something outside the code: a count against a raw file, a schedule against an observed quota, a percentage against a base rate, a metric against its own confidence interval.

That is the thread through the whole build, and it is why the verification script exists as a first-class stage rather than a final tidy-up. The failure mode of this kind of system is not a crash. It is a warning that is quietly wrong.

Where it stands

Twenty-four automated checks, twenty-three passing. Weather backfill at 21% of the sample and completing in roughly 2.7 days, after which the evaluation sample is fixed and model movement becomes meaningful for the first time. Everything else — warehouse, exposure, admin boundaries, model, scoring, dashboards, field app — is built and running.
